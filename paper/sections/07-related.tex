\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

AEP composes established mechanisms: leases, fenced compare-and-swap,
write-ahead logging, and an fsync barrier. We do not claim that durable intent
plus locking is new. The contribution is a capability-specific recovery
semantics for effects outside the coordinator's atomicity domain, and an
evaluation that separates detection from prevention.

\paragraph{Leases and fencing} Leases provide time-bounded mutual exclusion
under crash faults~\cite{gray1989leases}; Chubby made the pattern an
infrastructural service~\cite{burrows2006chubby}, and the Redis lock
pattern~\cite{redis-locks} is a commodity form. A lease holder may stall past
expiry and write afterwards, so a monotonic fencing token is also
required~\cite{kleppmann2016locking}. AEP applies that standard remedy to its
state store. B1 and B2 show its boundary: fencing protects the store, not an
external endpoint that does not interpret the token (\cref{tab:outcomes}).

\paragraph{Logging, outboxes, and long-lived transactions} The
expected-version CAS is optimistic concurrency control~\cite{kung1981optimistic}.
ARIES writes intent before an action that the same recovery domain can undo or
redo~\cite{mohan1992aries}; AEP instead records an action performed by a
foreign endpoint that may support neither operation. The transactional-outbox
pattern likewise stores a message before a relay publishes it, but explicitly
permits the relay to publish twice after a crash and therefore assumes an
idempotent consumer~\cite{richardson-transactional-outbox}. B3 is structurally
outbox-like, with a no-re-entry rule in the measured runner; it does not make
the remote mutation transactional. Sagas are appropriate when a compensating
action exists~\cite{garciamolina1987sagas}. AEP addresses the case in which no
compensation exists, or deciding whether to compensate first requires the
missing outcome.

The expired IETF HTTPAPI working-group draft for an \texttt{Idempotency-Key}
header documents the cooperative alternative: a server can make non-idempotent
\texttt{POST} and \texttt{PATCH} requests safely retryable by interpreting a
client key~\cite{jena2025idempotencykey}. AEP's premise is precisely the
endpoint that does not implement such a contract. Helland describes the
broader consequences of operating without distributed transactions and the
centrality of idempotence~\cite{helland2007apostate,helland2012idempotence}.

\paragraph{What an idempotency-key contract requires of the server} Because the
paper's premise is an endpoint that offers no such contract, it is worth being
precise about what one costs the server rather than treating it as a header.
The draft places the burden squarely on the resource: it must manage the key's
lifecycle, it \textsc{must} publish an idempotency specification including any
expiration policy, and on a repeat it \textsc{should} respond with the result of
the previously completed operation, success or error, rather than executing
again~\cite{jena2025idempotencykey}. Storing the key is the least of it; the
server must also retain the \emph{outcome}, scope the key, and decide how long
the guarantee lasts.

Notably the draft mandates little beyond publication --- it asks servers to
declare a policy rather than prescribing retention or scope --- and the deployed
contracts show why that matters. Stripe saves the status code and body of the
first request for a key and returns that same result on a repeat, including
\texttt{500}s, prunes keys after at least 24 hours, and rejects a reused key
whose parameters differ~\cite{stripe-idempotency}. Adyen returns the original
response without reprocessing, scopes keys to the company account, does not
cross-check duplicates across regional endpoints, and holds keys for seven to
fourteen days~\cite{adyen-idempotency}. The same header name therefore carries
materially different guarantees: a retry that is protected at one provider is
outside the window at another, and the caller cannot tell from the protocol.
These are specifications and vendor documentation rather than research, and are
cited as such --- the claim is about what the contract requires, for which the
normative text is the primary source. AEP assumes the case beneath all of them:
no key is interpreted at all. What the deployed contracts add is that even
cooperation is bounded, so a caller's obligation to reason about an unsettled
effect does not disappear where a key exists --- it merely shrinks to the
retention window.

\paragraph{Why not two-phase commit} The obvious objection to this whole
framing is that atomic commit already solves it, so it is worth saying exactly
what that would require of the endpoint. Under 2PC each participant durably
logs its own state transitions to stable storage and answers the coordinator
from that log during recovery~\cite{mohan1986rstar}. A participant that has
voted to commit then enters an uncertainty window in which it can neither
commit nor abort on its own authority, and must block --- holding the prepared
state --- until the coordinator is heard from again~\cite{skeen1981nonblocking}.
Both are obligations on the \emph{participant}. A legacy endpoint that accepts a
request and returns a response offers none of them: there is no prepare, no
vote, and no way to ask it to hold a decision open. The barrier is not that 2PC
is expensive here; it is that the protocol has no participant to run.

Nor is this a counsel of despair about distributed transactions in general.
Spanner shows the obligations can be met at global scale, with a
2PC implementation integrated with replication and concurrency control and a
time API that bounds clock uncertainty~\cite{corbett2012spanner} --- but it is
built by an operator who controls every participant and can equip each one.
That is the condition AEP assumes away. Helland's account of building without
distributed transactions describes the same retreat from the application
side~\cite{helland2007apostate,helland2012idempotence}: when enrolment is not
available, the burden moves to the caller, and idempotence becomes the pivot on
which correctness turns. AEP is what is left when the endpoint does not supply
that pivot either.

\paragraph{What compensation assumes} Compensation is the standard answer once
atomic commit is off the table, and it rests on a premise worth stating.
Gray observes that a committed transaction cannot be abrogated: its effects can
only be altered by running a further transaction, which is why the remedy after
commit is compensation rather than rollback~\cite{gray1981transaction}. Korth
et al.\ formalise this, defining compensating transactions that undo committed
work without cascading aborts, with conflict expressed as failure of operations
to commute~\cite{korth1990compensating}. Sagas apply the idea to long-running
activities by pairing each step with a compensating step~\cite{garciamolina1987sagas}.

Every one of these presupposes two facts about the effect. The first is that it
\emph{happened}: a compensating step is chosen and applied because a committed
effect is known to exist. The second is that a semantically inverse operation
exists and may itself be applied. AEP's endpoint reliably supplies neither. In
the \textsc{no-readback} class the first is unobtainable by construction --- no
participant can establish after a crash whether the mutation was applied --- and
compensating for an effect that may not have occurred is itself a mutation with
the same problem. That is why declared ambiguity is terminal here rather than a
prelude to compensation: it is the honest report when the fact compensation
needs cannot be recovered.

\paragraph{Locks with intent and durable execution} Olive is the closest
mechanistic antecedent. Its locks with intent durably associate code with a
lock and provide exactly-once execution despite client failures and concurrent
duplicate executions~\cite{setty2016olive}. A write-ahead intent as a checked
precondition of execution is therefore prior work, not AEP's novelty. Olive's
guarantee is realized through operations made recoverable within its cloud
storage abstraction. AEP starts where that atomicity scope ends: the effect is
performed by a legacy endpoint, and in the \textsc{no-readback} class no
participant can later establish whether it happened. AEP consequently offers
no exactly-once claim; it terminates in declared ambiguity and prohibits
automatic re-dispatch.

Beldi~\cite{zhang2020beldi}, Durable
Functions~\cite{burckhardt2021durable}, Boki~\cite{jia2021boki}, and
ExoFlow~\cite{zhuang2023exoflow} develop transactional or replay-based durable
execution. Their guarantees are appropriate when an effect is idempotent,
transactional, compensable, or otherwise recoverable. Temporal states the
operational premise directly: activities should be idempotent because they may
execute more than once~\cite{temporal-activity-definition}; setting Maximum
Attempts to one selects the opposite retry policy~\cite{temporal-retry}, and
the engine's own account of when an activity is considered failed rests on
timeouts and heartbeats rather than on any fact about the
endpoint~\cite{temporal-activity-failures}. Our B4 and B4b controls instantiate
these two policies against the same non-cooperative endpoint. They are semantic
controls, not claims about those products' complete implementations.

That distinction is no longer only a caveat. We subsequently ran the production
engine itself against the same endpoint, in both configurations, at the one
crash point the engine and our controls can both be cut at
(\cref{sec:threats}). \emph{The direction transferred and the magnitudes did
not}: unlimited attempts duplicated and lost nothing, one attempt lost and
duplicated nothing --- each landing in the corner \cref{tab:related} assigns it
--- while the rates were several times below B4's and B4b's at the same crash
point, and neither configuration declared ambiguity in any run. The comparison
covers one crash point of five, is conditional on a fixed activity timeout the
controls have no analogue for, and rests on frozen control cells an order of
magnitude thinner than the engine's; it is reported with those bounds in
\cref{sec:threats} and no rate in this paper should be read as a product's.

The ablation supplies AEP's narrower empirical delta. B3 and AEP-full share
the pre-dispatch record and no-re-entry transition but differ on the durability
barrier. We observe no difference in their crashed-regime detection outcomes,
bounded by pooling the capability classes: both zero-event rates fall within
$[0,\AblationZeroUpperRun{}\%]$ at joint coverage of at least 90\%
(\cref{sec:eval-detection}). The Redis-kill experiment isolates a prevention
effect. We found no prior evaluation that measures this detection/prevention
decomposition with an independent external-effect ledger.

\paragraph{Agent execution reliability} Three contemporaneous systems overlap
substantially and delimit the claim. Verified Tool Calls formalizes non-atomic
agent tool failures and uses postcondition verification, verify-before-retry,
and idempotency keys in a simulated fault environment~\cite{mansoor2026verified}.
Its verifier requires a queryable postcondition; without server-side
idempotency, it characterizes the remaining race as best-effort. AEP evaluates
the authoritative, positive-only, and absent-readback cases separately and
uses terminal ambiguity for the last case.

LogAct durably exposes intentions in a shared log before execution and supports
recovery and policy voting for open-ended agent control flow
~\cite{balakrishnan2026logact}. It is broader in agent-state-machine structure
and isolation; AEP is narrower in studying what can be concluded about one
non-idempotent external mutation after an interruption. The Sovereign Execution
Broker (SEB) reserves certificate nonces before mutation, disallows retry after
possible invocation unless durable or target-side evidence proves otherwise,
and provides a mandatory broker boundary with cryptographic certificates
~\cite{he2026seb}. That boundary is stronger than AEP's trusted-process guard.
AEP's distinct evidence is the three endpoint-capability classes, terminal
declared ambiguity, real \texttt{SIGKILL} and Redis durability faults, and the
B3/AEP ablation. ACRFence addresses semantic rollback during agent
checkpoint-restore~\cite{zheng2026acrfence}; it is adjacent rather than a basis
for ranking which work is ``nearest.''

\paragraph{Durability acknowledgements and what they are worth} AEP's barrier
withholds dispatch until the store acknowledges the intent durable, so the
protocol is only as strong as that acknowledgement. A body of storage work
establishes that the acknowledgement can be false. Rebello et al.\ show that
none of five widely deployed applications --- PostgreSQL, LMDB, LevelDB, SQLite
and Redis --- handles \texttt{fsync} failure sufficiently, and that on Linux the
affected pages are marked clean regardless of whether writeback
succeeded~\cite{rebello2020fsync}; the operational discovery that a successful
\texttt{fsync} does not mean the data reached persistent storage was made in
PostgreSQL and reported at the time~\cite{corbet2018fsync}. Ganesan et al.\
find that eight distributed storage systems, Redis among them, do not
consistently use redundancy to recover from file-system faults, so a single
fault can produce data loss or unavailability~\cite{ganesan2017redundancy}.
Alagappan et al.\ show that recovery which ignores protocol-level knowledge
loses data in replicated logs, and repair it for
Raft-style~\cite{ongaro2014raft} systems~\cite{alagappan2018par}.

We place our own durability result in that line rather than beside it:
\cref{sec:threats} reports a barrier that dispatched on an acknowledgement which
was already impossible to honour, because the underlying device discarded the
write and reported success. The protocol behaved exactly as specified on an
input that was false. What this literature supplies is the reason that is a
property of the storage stack rather than a defect peculiar to our
implementation --- and the reason a barrier's guarantee must be stated as
conditional on the acknowledgement it rests on.

\paragraph{Exactly-once state versus exactly-once effect} Consistent recovery
for distributed computation is classically obtained by snapshotting global
state~\cite{chandy1985snapshots}, and modern dataflow engines make that
practical by injecting barriers into the stream and persisting operator state
asynchronously~\cite{carbone2015abs}. The guarantee these mechanisms provide is
that \emph{internal state} is restored to a consistent cut. An external
mutation already performed is not inside that cut and is not undone by
restoring it, which is why exactly-once processing is routinely paired with a
transactional or idempotent sink. AEP's endpoint is neither, and in the
\textsc{no-readback} class it cannot even be interrogated after the fact --- so
the snapshot line of work and AEP are solving adjacent problems on opposite
sides of the atomicity boundary. This is also the end-to-end
argument~\cite{saltzer1984endtoend} in an uncomfortable form: the check that
would settle whether the effect happened belongs at the endpoints, and the
endpoint that would have to perform it is precisely the one that cannot.

\paragraph{Exactly-once delivery, and what is provided instead} The paper
declines the term ``exactly-once,'' and should say what it is declining. The
obstacle is epistemic rather than engineering. For a sender and a receiver to
agree that a message was delivered, each acknowledgement requires its own
acknowledgement; Halpern and Moses formalise this as the unattainability of
common knowledge when communication is not guaranteed, and connect it to the
coordinated-attack problem~\cite{halpern1990knowledge}. No protocol removes that
regress, which is why exactly-once \emph{delivery} is not on offer from any
system.

What systems provide instead is exactly-once \emph{processing}, obtained by
enrolling the destination. Kafka couples an idempotent producer protocol with
transactions so that output records, offset commits and state updates are
committed atomically, and the read--process--write cycle either takes effect
once or not at all~\cite{wang2021kafka}. That guarantee is real, and it is real
\emph{because} the sink is inside the transaction: the records live in a log the
system controls. The same construction is why the snapshot mechanisms above are
usually paired with a transactional sink.

This is the distinction the paper needs, and it is the one the term usually
obscures. A message delivered once is not an external mutation performed once.
Where the destination can be enrolled --- a Kafka topic, a database in the same
commit scope --- exactly-once processing is available and AEP is unnecessary.
Where it cannot, no amount of retry discipline on the caller's side reconstructs
it, and the residual is not a weaker delivery guarantee but an unanswered
question about an effect. AEP occupies exactly that gap.

\paragraph{Fault-injection lineage} AEP's named crash boundaries and
independent oracle follow a mature crash-consistency methodology. ALICE
constructs possible post-crash storage states and checks application
invariants~\cite{pillai2014alice}; Torturing Databases combines controlled
power-fault injection with record/replay and cross-layer diagnosis
~\cite{zheng2014torturing}; and CrashMonkey/Ace exhaustively test bounded
workloads at persistence boundaries~\cite{mohan2018crashmonkey}. AEP applies
this lineage to a caller-to-endpoint protocol: real process kills at named
instruction boundaries, a separately written provider ledger, and a
\texttt{dm-flakey} write-loss probe. The harness is an evaluation instrument,
not a claimed fault-injection contribution.

\paragraph{Revised novelty position} AEP's claim is not the invention of
write-ahead intent, locking, or fail-closed interposition. It is the measured
composition for endpoints with authoritative, positive-only, or no readback:
capability-specific recovery; a terminal declared-ambiguity outcome when no
query can settle the effect; real process and durability-fault evaluation with
an independent ground-truth ledger; and an ablation that assigns detection to
the pre-dispatch record plus no re-entry, and prevention to the durability
barrier.
