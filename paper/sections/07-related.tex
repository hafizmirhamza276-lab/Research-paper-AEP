\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

AEP composes established mechanisms: leases, fenced compare-and-swap,
write-ahead logging, and an fsync barrier. We do not claim that durable intent
plus locking is new. The contribution is a capability-specific recovery
semantics for effects outside the coordinator's atomicity domain, and an
evaluation that separates detection from prevention.

Prior work is therefore organised here by the three questions that decide
whether it applies to a non-cooperative endpoint. \textbf{First, what does it
require the endpoint to supply?} A mechanism may need idempotence, an
idempotency key, enrolment in a transaction, or a compensating action, and one
that needs any of these does not apply where none can be obtained. \textbf{Second, can it
report \emph{unknown}?} That is, does it have a terminal outcome meaning
``an effect may exist and I cannot establish whether it does'', as distinct from
success or failure. \textbf{Third, what residual does it leave when its stated
assumption fails?} The candidates are a duplicate effect, a lost effect, a
blocked participant, a compensation gap, or a declared ambiguity. The three subsections below are the
three questions, and every system discussed is placed against all three. The
same placement is tabulated in the supplementary material, which lists fourteen
mechanisms and AEP against these columns; of the fourteen, only AEP answers
\emph{no} to the first question and \emph{yes} to the second.

\subsection{What the endpoint must supply}

\paragraph{Leases and fencing} Leases provide time-bounded mutual exclusion
under crash faults~\cite{gray1989leases}, as an infrastructural
service~\cite{burrows2006chubby}, as a coordination kernel for client-side
locking~\cite{hunt2010zookeeper}, and in commodity form~\cite{redis-locks}. A
lease holder may stall past expiry and write afterwards, so a monotonic fencing
token is also required.

Chubby states both the hazard and the remedy in primary form, which matters
because AEP's guard depends on the remedy and because the requirement it imposes
is the one this paper cannot satisfy. A client holding a lock may issue a request
and then fail, a second client may acquire the lock and act, and the first
request may arrive afterwards. The remedy is a \emph{sequencer}: the lock
name, a generation number that changes on every transition from free to held,
and the acquisition mode. The client passes it to the file server,
\emph{and the file server is expected to validate it}~\cite{burrows2006chubby}.
That validation requirement is the crux: a fencing token protects a resource only
if the resource checks it, which makes fencing a property of the \emph{resource}
rather than of the lock service. Chubby concedes the point by shipping a fallback
for servers that cannot be modified to validate sequencers: \emph{lock-delay},
which merely withholds a lock for a bounded period after an abnormal release and
which that paper calls imperfect. It is the closest prior art to AEP's setting
and it is a timing heuristic, not a guarantee. Kleppmann's analysis remains the
clearest statement of the failure mode in the commodity Redis setting and is
cited as the practitioner account it is~\cite{kleppmann2016locking}. B1 and B2
measure exactly this boundary: fencing protects the store, not an endpoint that
never interprets the token (\cref{tab:outcomes}).

\paragraph{Logging, outboxes, and what compensation assumes} The
expected-version CAS is optimistic concurrency control~\cite{kung1981optimistic}.
ARIES writes intent before an action that the same recovery domain can undo or
redo~\cite{mohan1992aries}; AEP instead records an action performed by a foreign
endpoint that may support neither operation. The transactional-outbox pattern
likewise stores a message before a relay publishes it, but explicitly permits the
relay to publish twice after a crash and therefore assumes an idempotent
consumer~\cite{richardson-transactional-outbox}. B3 is structurally outbox-like,
with a no-re-entry rule in the measured runner; it does not make the remote
mutation transactional.

Compensation is the standard answer once atomicity is unavailable: a
committed effect cannot be abrogated, only altered by a further
transaction~\cite{gray1981transaction}, formalised as compensating transactions
that undo committed work without cascading aborts~\cite{korth1990compensating}
and applied to long-running activities by pairing each step with a compensating
one~\cite{garciamolina1987sagas}. All three presuppose two facts about the
effect: that it \emph{happened}, and that a semantically inverse operation
exists and may be applied. AEP's endpoint reliably supplies neither. In the
\textsc{no-readback} class the first is unobtainable by construction, and
compensating for an effect that may not have occurred is itself a mutation with
the same problem, which is why declared ambiguity is terminal here rather
than a prelude to compensation. Helland describes the broader consequences of
operating without distributed transactions and the centrality of
idempotence~\cite{helland2007apostate,helland2012idempotence}.

\paragraph{What an idempotency-key contract requires of the server} The expired
IETF HTTPAPI working-group draft for an \texttt{Idempotency-Key} header
documents the cooperative alternative, and because AEP's premise is an endpoint
offering no such contract it is worth being precise about what one costs the
server rather than treating it as a header. The draft places the burden squarely
on the resource: it must manage the key's lifecycle, it \textsc{must} publish an
idempotency specification including any expiration policy, and on a repeat it
\textsc{should} respond with the result of the previously completed operation,
success or error, rather than executing
again~\cite{jena2025idempotencykey}. Storing the key is the least of it; the
server must also retain the \emph{outcome}, scope the key, and decide how long
the guarantee lasts.

The draft mandates little beyond publication, asking servers to
declare a policy rather than prescribing retention or scope, and the deployed
contracts show why that matters. Stripe saves the status code and body of the
first request for a key and returns that same result on a repeat, including
\texttt{500}s, prunes keys after at least 24 hours, and rejects a reused key
whose parameters differ~\cite{stripe-idempotency}. Adyen returns the original
response without reprocessing, scopes keys to the company account, does not
cross-check duplicates across regional endpoints, and holds keys for seven to
fourteen days~\cite{adyen-idempotency}. The same header name therefore carries
materially different guarantees: a retry protected at one provider is outside the
window at another, and the caller cannot tell from the protocol. These are
specifications and vendor documentation rather than research, and are cited as
such. The claim is about what the contract requires, for which the normative
text is the primary source. AEP assumes the case beneath all of them: no key is
interpreted at all. What the deployed contracts add is that even cooperation is
bounded, so a caller's obligation to reason about an unsettled effect does not
disappear where a key exists; it shrinks to the retention window.

\paragraph{Why not two-phase commit} The obvious objection is that atomic commit
already solves this, so it is worth saying exactly what that would require of the
endpoint. Under 2PC each participant durably logs its own state transitions to
stable storage and answers the coordinator from that log during
recovery~\cite{mohan1986rstar}. A participant that has voted to commit then
enters an uncertainty window in which it can neither commit nor abort on its own
authority, and must block, holding the prepared state, until the
coordinator is heard from again~\cite{skeen1981nonblocking}. Both are obligations
on the \emph{participant}, and a legacy endpoint that accepts a request and
returns a response offers none of them: no prepare, no vote, and no way to ask it
to hold a decision open. The barrier is not that 2PC is expensive here; it is
that the protocol has no participant to run.

Nor is this a counsel of despair about distributed transactions. Spanner shows
the obligations can be met at global scale, with a 2PC implementation integrated
with replication and concurrency control and a time API that bounds clock
uncertainty~\cite{corbett2012spanner}. It is, however, built by an operator
who controls every participant and can equip each one. That is the condition AEP
assumes away, and when enrolment is unavailable the burden moves to the caller
and idempotence becomes the pivot on which correctness turns. AEP is what is left
when the endpoint does not supply that pivot either.

\subsection{Whether it can report \emph{unknown}}

\paragraph{Locks with intent, and the durable-orchestration line} Olive is the
closest mechanistic antecedent. Its locks with intent durably associate code with
a lock and provide exactly-once execution despite client failures and concurrent
duplicate executions~\cite{setty2016olive}. A write-ahead intent as a checked
precondition of execution is therefore prior work, not AEP's novelty. Olive's
guarantee is realized through operations made recoverable within its cloud
storage abstraction; AEP starts where that atomicity scope ends, and consequently
offers no exactly-once claim: it terminates in declared ambiguity and
prohibits automatic re-dispatch.

The idea is older than the current generation of products. Workflow management
systems already specified business processes and generated implementations that
recorded progress durably across long-running
activities~\cite{georgakopoulos1995workflow}; the recovery semantics they relied
on were the compensation models above, which is why sagas sit underneath that
literature rather than beside it. What the current generation added is a sharper
boundary, and each system states the same assumption in its own documentation.
Cadence introduced the framing: workflow code is fault-oblivious and may not call
an external API directly, only orchestrate \emph{activities}, and because the
service does not recover an activity's state, activity failure is expected and
retried~\cite{cadence-workflows}. Restate journals each step and replays after a
crash, skipping completed steps, and it names the residual exactly: if a side
effect completes but the failure prevents its result being recorded, replay runs
it again, so side effects are required to be
idempotent~\cite{restate-durable-execution}. AWS states it normatively: a Step
Functions workflow conforms to an at-least-once execution model in which each
step may run more than once, and step-level idempotency remains the caller's
responsibility~\cite{aws-stepfunctions-bestpractices}; its durable execution SDK
turns the trade into an explicit per-step knob, recommending at-least-once for
idempotent operations and at-most-once for externally side-effecting ones such as
charging a card or posting to a non-idempotent
API~\cite{aws-durable-execution-idempotency}. That last sentence is this paper's
problem statement, written by a vendor. DBOS takes the other route, placing
system state in a distributed transactional DBMS so that services are ordinary
database operations, recoverable as such~\cite{skiadopoulos2022dbos}, with
recoverability again purchased by keeping the effect inside the transactional
domain, the same boundary Olive draws.

Four systems in this line draw that boundary in different places. Beldi extends
Olive's atomic logging to stateful serverless functions and adds a two-phase
commit variant across them~\cite{zhang2020beldi}; the atomicity covers Beldi's
own data structures and functions that speak its commit protocol, so an endpoint
implementing neither is outside it. Boki gives functions a shared log and builds
durability, consistency and fault tolerance on that primitive~\cite{jia2021boki};
state living in the log is recoverable, and an effect performed elsewhere is not
in the log to recover. Durable Functions replays the orchestration from its
history and re-invokes activities, safe when the activity can be
re-run~\cite{burckhardt2021durable}. ExoFlow is closest in spirit to this paper:
it decouples recovery from execution and asks the author to annotate each task's
semantics, including nondeterminism, so the system can choose a recovery strategy
per task~\cite{zhuang2023exoflow}. That is a declaration about the task by
someone who knows it, whereas AEP's declarations are about the \emph{endpoint's}
capacity to answer after the fact, which a caller cannot annotate its way out of.

The axis is not feature coverage but what each assumes of the invoked step: that
it is idempotent, compensable, or inside the same transactional domain. Where
that holds these systems are correct and AEP is unnecessary; AEP's endpoint
satisfies none of the three. Temporal states the operational premise directly:
activities should be idempotent because they may execute more than
once~\cite{temporal-activity-definition}; setting Maximum Attempts to one selects
the opposite retry policy~\cite{temporal-retry}, and the engine's own account of
when an activity is considered failed rests on timeouts and heartbeats rather
than on any fact about the endpoint~\cite{temporal-activity-failures}. Our B4 and
B4b controls instantiate these two policies against the same non-cooperative
endpoint. They are semantic controls, not claims about those products' complete
implementations.

That distinction is no longer only a caveat. We subsequently ran the production
engine itself against the same endpoint, in both configurations, at the one crash
point the engine and our controls can both be cut at (\cref{sec:eval-rq1}).
\emph{The direction transferred and the magnitudes did not}: unlimited attempts
duplicated and lost nothing, and one attempt lost and duplicated nothing, each
landing in the corner the supplementary comparison assigns it. The rates were
several times below B4's and B4b's at the same crash point, and neither
configuration declared ambiguity in any run. The comparison covers one crash
point of five, is conditional on a fixed activity timeout the controls have no
analogue for, and rests on frozen control cells an order of magnitude thinner
than the engine's; it is reported with those bounds in \cref{sec:eval-rq1}, our
measured comparison is with one engine and does not transfer to the others, and
no rate in this paper should be read as a product's.

The ablation supplies AEP's narrower empirical delta. B3 and AEP-full share the
pre-dispatch record and no-re-entry transition but differ on the durability
barrier. We observe no difference in their crashed-regime detection outcomes,
bounded by pooling the capability classes: both zero-event rates fall within
$[0,\AblationZeroUpperRun{}\%]$ at joint coverage of at least 90\%
(\cref{sec:eval-detection}). The Redis-kill experiment isolates a prevention
effect. We found no prior evaluation that measures this detection/prevention
decomposition with an independent external-effect ledger.

\paragraph{Agent execution reliability} Three contemporaneous systems overlap
substantially and delimit the claim. Verified Tool Calls formalizes non-atomic
agent tool failures and uses postcondition verification, verify-before-retry,
and idempotency keys in a simulated fault environment~\cite{mansoor2026verified}.
Its verifier requires a queryable postcondition; without server-side
idempotency, it characterizes the remaining race as best-effort. AEP evaluates
the authoritative, positive-only, and absent-readback cases separately and
uses terminal ambiguity for the last case.

Three contemporaneous systems bound the comparison. LogAct durably exposes
intentions in a shared log before execution~\cite{balakrishnan2026logact},
broader in agent-state-machine structure where AEP is narrower: what can be
concluded about one non-idempotent external mutation after an interruption. The
Sovereign Execution Broker reserves certificate nonces before mutation and
refuses retry after possible invocation absent durable or target-side
evidence~\cite{he2026seb}; its mandatory broker boundary with cryptographic
certificates is stronger than AEP's trusted-process guard. ACRFence addresses
semantic rollback during agent checkpoint-restore~\cite{zheng2026acrfence},
adjacent rather than a basis for ranking which work is ``nearest.'' AEP's
distinct evidence is the three endpoint-capability classes, terminal declared
ambiguity, real \texttt{SIGKILL} and Redis durability faults, and the B3/AEP
ablation.

\subsection{The residual when the assumption fails}

\paragraph{Durability acknowledgements and what they are worth} AEP's barrier
withholds dispatch until the store acknowledges the intent durable, so the
protocol is only as strong as that acknowledgement, and a body of storage work
establishes that the acknowledgement can be false. Rebello et al.\ show that none
of five widely deployed applications (PostgreSQL, LMDB, LevelDB, SQLite and
Redis) handles \texttt{fsync} failure sufficiently, and that on Linux the
affected pages are marked clean regardless of whether writeback
succeeded~\cite{rebello2020fsync}; the operational discovery that a successful
\texttt{fsync} does not mean the data reached persistent storage was made in
PostgreSQL and reported at the time~\cite{corbet2018fsync}. Ganesan et al.\ find
that eight distributed storage systems, Redis among them, do not consistently use
redundancy to recover from file-system faults, so a single fault can produce data
loss or unavailability~\cite{ganesan2017redundancy}. Alagappan et al.\ show that
recovery ignoring protocol-level knowledge loses data in replicated logs, and
repair it for Raft-style~\cite{ongaro2014raft} systems~\cite{alagappan2018par}.

We place our own durability result in that line rather than beside it:
\cref{sec:eval-writeloss-cell} reports a barrier that dispatched on an
acknowledgement which
was already impossible to honour, because the underlying device discarded the
write and reported success. The protocol behaved exactly as specified on an input
that was false. What this literature supplies is the reason that is a property of
the storage stack rather than a defect peculiar to our implementation, and the
reason a barrier's guarantee must be stated as conditional on the acknowledgement
it rests on.

\paragraph{Exactly-once: state, delivery, and effect} The paper declines the term
``exactly-once,'' and should say what it is declining, because the term covers
three different things. Consistent recovery for distributed computation is
classically obtained by snapshotting global state~\cite{chandy1985snapshots}, and
modern dataflow engines make that practical by injecting barriers into the stream
and persisting operator state asynchronously~\cite{carbone2015abs}. What those
mechanisms guarantee is that \emph{internal state} is restored to a consistent
cut; an external mutation already performed is not inside that cut and is not
undone by restoring it.

Exactly-once \emph{delivery} is not on offer from any system, and the obstacle is
epistemic rather than engineering. For a sender and a receiver to agree that a
message was delivered, each acknowledgement requires its own acknowledgement;
Halpern and Moses formalise this as the unattainability of common knowledge when
communication is not guaranteed, and connect it to the coordinated-attack
problem~\cite{halpern1990knowledge}. No protocol removes that regress. What
systems provide instead is exactly-once \emph{processing}, obtained by enrolling
the destination: Kafka couples an idempotent producer protocol with transactions
so that output records, offset commits and state updates commit atomically, and
the read--process--write cycle either takes effect once or not at
all~\cite{wang2021kafka}. That guarantee is real, and it is real \emph{because}
the sink is inside the transaction, which is also why the snapshot mechanisms
above are paired with a transactional sink.

This is the distinction the paper needs and the one the term usually obscures. A
message delivered once is not an external mutation performed once. Where the
destination can be enrolled (a Kafka topic, a database in the same commit
scope), exactly-once processing is available and AEP is unnecessary. Where it
cannot, no retry discipline on the caller's side reconstructs it, and the
residual is not a weaker delivery guarantee but an unanswered question about an
effect. This is also the end-to-end argument~\cite{saltzer1984endtoend} in an
uncomfortable form: the check that would settle whether the effect happened
belongs at the endpoints, and the endpoint that would have to perform it is
exactly the one that cannot.

\paragraph{Fault-injection lineage} AEP's named crash boundaries and
independent oracle follow a mature crash-consistency
methodology~\cite{pillai2014alice,zheng2014torturing,mohan2018crashmonkey,bornholt2016ferrite},
which constructs post-crash states, injects power faults, or specifies a
system's crash model as litmus tests. The last is unavailable here in the
direction that matters: a legacy endpoint publishes no crash-consistency model
to write litmus tests against, which is the problem rather than an oversight.

Three distributed-systems methods bound the comparison. FATE explores
\emph{sequences} of failures against declarative recovery
specifications~\cite{gunawi2011fate}; our cells inject one fault at one named
boundary, trading coverage of combinations for an exact statement of where the
single fault landed. Lineage-driven injection reasons backwards from a correct
outcome to the faults that could have prevented it, over a model of dropped
messages, failed nodes and partitions~\cite{alvaro2015ldfi}, a model that
excludes crash recovery, the class this paper is about. Elle infers a dependency
graph from what clients saw~\cite{kingsbury2020elle}; the contrast is the
oracle, since our provider ledger is written independently of the system under
test and is never consulted by it. AEP applies this lineage to a
caller-to-endpoint protocol, and the harness is an evaluation instrument rather
than a claimed contribution.


Read down the three columns, the position is this. AEP's claim is not the
invention of write-ahead intent, locking, or fail-closed interposition. It is the
measured composition for endpoints with authoritative, positive-only, or no
readback: capability-specific recovery; a terminal declared-ambiguity outcome
when no query can settle the effect; real process and durability-fault evaluation
with an independent ground-truth ledger; and an ablation that assigns detection
to the pre-dispatch record plus no re-entry, and prevention to the durability
barrier.
