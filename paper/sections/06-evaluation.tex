\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] Under crashes, does AEP eliminate \emph{undetected}
      duplicates, and what does its residual ambiguity depend on?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] What does each mechanism contribute --- in particular, what
      does the durability barrier buy, and against which faults?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] What does the protocol cost?
\item[\textbf{RQ4}] How does recovery behave?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:eval-setup}

\paragraph{Systems} The six of \cref{tab:trilemma}, implemented as thin
variants over one connector, one workload driver and one oracle, so that only
the differences differ. Each carries a machine-readable descriptor that tests
check against the implementation, and a crash point that does not exist in a
given system is marked inapplicable and never run rather than silently skipped.

\paragraph{Faults} Workers are separate OS processes killed with real
\texttt{SIGKILL} at one of the six crash points of \cref{tab:crashpoints}. A
\emph{regime} is a named fault condition --- not a matrix dimension --- and we
report each separately because they are different experiments:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{crashed}: every execution is killed at the cell's crash point.
      This is the regime RQ1 is about.
\item \textbf{crash-free} (\texttt{p0}): nothing is injected. The only regime
      RQ3 may use.
\item \textbf{\texttt{redis-kill-preack}}: no worker crash; Redis is hard-killed
      with \texttt{docker kill -s KILL} at the instruction boundary between the
      intent CAS and the barrier acknowledgement, then restarted and verified.
      This is RQ2's ablation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Oracle} A standalone mock provider records every \emph{actually
applied} mutation in a SQLite ledger (WAL, \texttt{synchronous=FULL}, one
transaction per mutation). Duplicates are counted from that ledger, never from
the system's own opinion of itself. The analysis is permitted to read exactly
two files per run --- the event log and the oracle ledger --- and a source gate
that parses the analysis modules fails the build if they import anything that
could reach live protocol state.

\paragraph{Statistics} Rates are over executions, with cluster-aware bootstrap
95\% confidence intervals (10\,000 resamples, seeded) and Fisher's exact test
for rate comparisons. Seeds and repetition counts are recorded per run.

\paragraph{Two reporting rules we hold ourselves to} First, \emph{no pooled
table}. A rate computed across fault regimes is a property of how many runs of
each kind were collected, not of any system; our own analysis tool prints a
warning whenever more than one regime is present, and every number below names
its regime. Second, \emph{no absolute timing from an ungated host}. A run
contributes a duration only if the operator declared before the run that the
host cannot suspend \emph{and} the run's wall-versus-monotonic divergence stays
within tolerance. Both halves are necessary: a host that merely was not idle
long enough to suspend is indistinguishable from one that cannot. Under this
gate, \emph{zero} runs collected before this rule existed contribute a timing
number, and none does.

The gap between B1/B2 and B0 is small and that is the finding: a lease and a

\paragraph{A durable log is not enough --- and this is the sharpest statement

is necessary and it is not sufficient, because the record cannot contain the
fact that matters --- whether the provider applied the effect. What separates
B3 and AEP-full from B4 is not durability but the \emph{absence of an edge back
into the dispatching state}: the transition table has no path that re-enters
\textsc{about-to-fire}, checked inside the atomic script
(\cref{fig:statemachine}).

\subsubsection{The residual is set by the endpoint, not the protocol}

AEP's declared-ambiguity column is \AepAmbAuth{} on \textsc{auth},
\AepAmbPosOnly{} on \textsc{pos-only} and \AepAmbNoReadback{} on
\textsc{none}. That ordering is not a defect that better engineering would
remove; it is \cref{tab:capabilities} showing through. Where the endpoint can
prove absence, recovery resolves everything and nothing is left ambiguous.
Where it can only confirm presence, absence is uninformative and the honest
answer is ``I do not know''. Where it cannot be queried at all, no amount of
protocol recovers the fact.

\Cref{tab:ambiguity-by-crashpoint} decomposes this further, and it is where the
protocol's conservatism becomes visible.

\input{generated/table-ambiguity-by-crashpoint}

Two cells carry the argument. At \texttt{before\_intent\_write} the rate is
$0$ in every capability class: the crash precedes the intent, nothing was
promised, and there is nothing to be ambiguous about. At
\texttt{after\_barrier\_before\_dispatch} it is at its maximum --- and that is
the crash point at which \emph{no effect can possibly exist}, because the
worker died before sending anything. AEP declares ambiguity most often exactly
where it is least warranted by the world. That is the false-positive ambiguity
of \cref{sec:p2}, and its direction is the whole design: the protocol's errors
are toward saying ``I might have done something'' when it did not, never toward
silence when it did. The cells where an effect \emph{does} typically exist ---
\texttt{mid\_dispatch} and \texttt{after\_response\_before\_resolution} under
\textsc{pos-only} --- show low ambiguity, because a positive read-back
confirms the effect and the intent resolves.

\subsection{RQ2 --- what each mechanism contributes}
\label{sec:eval-rq2}

B3 is AEP-full with the \texttt{WAITAOF} barrier removed and nothing else
removed. On the crashed regime it is indistinguishable from AEP-full: both
record zero undetected duplicates. That is not a null result about the barrier;
it is a statement that the \emph{worker-crash} fault class cannot separate
them, and it sent us looking for the fault that can.

\subsubsection{A negative result that narrows our own claim}
\label{sec:eval-negative}

The intuitive story is that the barrier protects the write-ahead record from
being lost on a crash. We tested that premise before building on it, and it is
false for the fault we and most readers would first reach for.

Ten trials, each phase-aligning the \texttt{everysec} timer with a
\texttt{WAITAOF} so the window under test is as wide as it can be, writing a key
\emph{without} waiting for it, hard-killing the container with
\texttt{docker kill -s KILL}, restarting, and reading back. Every kill landed
(\texttt{uptime} $=0$\,s afterwards in all ten) and every kill landed inside the
window (write-to-death $419$--$992$\,ms against a $1\,000$\,ms fsync period).
\textbf{Not one unfsynced write was lost: 0/10.}
% source: reports/raw/e1-durability-window.txt; Session 3B section C.2.

The mechanism is not subtle once seen. \texttt{appendfsync everysec} defers the
\texttt{fsync(2)}, \textbf{not} the \texttt{write(2)}: Redis writes the AOF
buffer to the operating system on every event-loop iteration. A
\texttt{SIGKILL} destroys the \emph{process}; the kernel and its page cache are
untouched, and a kernel that is still running flushes those bytes to disk. The
same result reappears at $n{=}60$ inside the ablation cells below, where a
canary write made immediately before each kill survived in
\AepKillCanary{} and \BthreeKillCanary{} runs.

\textbf{The consequence for the claim.} \texttt{WAITAOF} defends against loss of
the page cache --- host power failure, kernel panic, VM destruction --- and
\emph{not} against process death, because \texttt{appendonly yes} already
covers that. No process-level fault can separate B3 from AEP-full on the
durability of the write-ahead record, and we therefore do not claim it does.
The Redis documentation's ``you may lose 1 second of data if there is a
disaster''~\cite{redis-persistence} is correct; what our probe adds is that
``disaster'' must mean the host, and that the widely repeated reading of the
sentence as covering a crashed Redis process is wrong.

\subsubsection{The benefit that is measurable, and it is large}

The barrier buys a second thing, and that one is observable. If the barrier's
acknowledgement is a checked precondition of dispatch (\cref{sec:p2}), then
losing Redis \emph{during} the barrier must withhold the dispatch --- whereas
B3, which does not wait, proceeds.

We hard-kill Redis at \texttt{after\_intent\_before\_barrier}: the instruction
boundary between the intent CAS and the acknowledgement. AEP-full is inside
\texttt{WAITAOF} at that moment; B3 is not, because B3's ablation is precisely
that round trip. One execution per run, \AepKillRuns{} runs per system, no
worker crash --- the fault under study is Redis dying.

AEP-full's dispatch gate withheld the mutation in 20 of 30 runs; B3 put it on
the wire in 28 of 30. Fisher's exact test, two-tailed: $p = 1.9\times10^{-6}$.
% source: analysis/redis-kill-ablation.csv; Session 3B section C.5.
That is \textbf{18 real, non-idempotent effects} that AEP-full did not commit
and B3 did, on the same injected fault, bought by one round trip.

Two honest qualifications. First, \emph{AEP-full still applied 10}. The kill is
asynchronous and takes $400$--$1\,000$\,ms to land, so in roughly a third of
runs \texttt{WAITAOF} completed before Redis died and AEP-full held a genuine
acknowledgement and dispatched correctly. That is the race the fault creates,
not a protocol failure, and we report it as it came out rather than widening
the window until it looks better. Second, and for the same reason, \emph{the
effect size is a property of this host's \texttt{docker} latency}. A faster
kill pushes AEP-full toward 0 and widens the gap; a slower one narrows it. The
\emph{direction} is structural --- B3 cannot be protected by a barrier it does
not wait for --- but the magnitude should not be read as a constant of the
protocol.

Third: \emph{both systems declared ambiguity on all 30}. The endpoint is
\textsc{no-readback}, so neither can resolve what it does not know, and both
fail closed. The difference between them is not in what they \emph{say}; it is
in what they did to the outside world before saying it.

\subsection{RQ3 --- cost}
\label{sec:eval-rq3}

and the interesting quantity is the increment over it.

\textbf{The overhead is the barrier, and nothing else.} Everything the
write-ahead protocol does apart from waiting for \texttt{fsync} --- the intent
ledger, the vault, the request binding, the preflight, the recovery service ---
costs $28$\,ms on a two-second call, under $1.4\%$. The two \texttt{WAITAOF}
round trips cost $\approx 1\,967$\,ms between them, or $\approx 983$\,ms each.

That figure is a property of a \emph{configuration}, not of AEP. Under
\texttt{appendfsync everysec} a write must wait for the next scheduled fsync,
and those are one second apart; a mean wait of half a second per barrier, twice,
is precisely what we measure. B4 and B4b landing on the same figure with their
own two acknowledged appends is independent confirmation that the cost is the
barrier and not anything AEP does around it.

To show that the barrier's cost is a point on a curve rather than a constant,
we re-ran the crash-free AEP-full configuration against a Redis configured with
\texttt{appendfsync always}, changing nothing else.

\subsection{RQ4 --- recovery}
\label{sec:eval-rq4}

Recovery behaviour is reported through the outcome distribution rather than a
success rate, for a reason worth stating: ``recovery success'' means two
different things either side of whether a recovery service was running. Where
one ran, a crashed execution reaching a terminal classification was
\emph{recovered}. Where none ran --- B0, B1, B2, B4, B4b declare none --- the
same execution reached its classification because a supervisor ran the step
again, which is not recovery but the thing recovery exists to avoid. Our
analysis excludes runs without a recovery service from that metric rather than
scoring them zero or one, because both would be statements about a component
that was not present.

Within that scope: every AEP-full and B3 execution in the crashed regime
reached a terminal classification, and \emph{zero} reconciliation
disagreements were recorded across all runs --- every run's own event log
agreed with its independently written oracle ledger.
% source: analysis/coverage.json and the per-run reconcile step.
Recovery latency is not reported as an absolute figure: the E5 gate leaves too
few gated crashed runs to support a distribution, and \cref{sec:threats}
records this as a gap rather than filling it with ungated numbers.
