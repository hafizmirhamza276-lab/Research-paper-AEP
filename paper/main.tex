\IEEEtitleabstractindextext{%
\begin{abstract}
Many enterprise APIs are non-idempotent, accept no idempotency key, and cannot be
asked afterwards whether a mutation was applied. A caller that crashes
around such a call --- an autonomous agent, a workflow engine, a retrying job
--- has no safe option: retrying risks a second real-world effect nobody
observes, and not retrying risks an effect that exists and that no record
accounts for. We argue this is not a question of engineering quality but a
three-way trade whose third corner a system is obliged to make reachable:
converting silent failure into \emph{declared, durable, bounded ambiguity} an
operator can act on.

We present a fail-closed execution protocol in which every external side effect
is preceded by a durably acknowledged write-ahead intent, every state write is
fenced by lock ownership and an expected-version compare-and-swap in one atomic
script, and every unresolvable outcome halts and escalates rather than guessing.
We evaluate it against five baseline designs under real \texttt{SIGKILL} process
faults, across endpoint capabilities differing in what a read-back can settle.

Our central result separates two claims the write-ahead pattern is usually sold
as one. \emph{Detection} --- no undetected duplicate and no lost effect, leaving
a residual of declared ambiguity --- comes from the pre-dispatch record and a
transition table that forbids re-entry into dispatch, not from the durability
barrier. \emph{Prevention} is what the barrier contributes, and its evidence is
narrower: a single capability class, crash point and host. Because detection
does not depend on the barrier, its cost is a deployment choice rather than the
protocol's price.
\end{abstract}
